CHAPTER ONE: INTRODUCTION
1.1 Background of the Project
The current categorizations of requirements, which constitute the backbone of requirements engineering, present ambiguity and limited structural coherence. This inadequacy is mainly due to the absence of a reference framework for the definition of software elements and the software environment to which the requirements refer. In fact, given the enormous complexity of modern software programs, it is impossible to properly identify and categorize their requirements without a thorough understanding of the following elements:
software and its development and operation environments:
software: composed of functions, data, user interfaces and technical interfaces,
development environment: consists of the human support (developers), with two types of support, software and hardware, the procedural support for its development (development activities) and the cognitive context of its development (methods, techniques, artifacts and tools),
operation environment: consists of the human support (end users), with two types of support, software and hardware, the procedural support for its operation (operation activities of the encompassing system) and the cognitive context of its operation (concepts, goals and rules);
factors that affect software as well as its development and operation environments:
quality factors such as usability, reliability, safety and portability,
compliance factors to the various technological, economic and cultural constraints.
The new systemic approach to categorization and requirements modeling, named SMART (Systemic Approach to caTegorizing and Modeling Requirements), takes into account the previous elements to provide the following:
at the conceptual level:
a new R2F (requirements reference framework) framework, which makes it possible to precisely define all aspects related to the software and to its development and operation environments; these latter constitute the subjects of requirements as well as the quality and conformity factors that affect them.
a new definition of requirements based on R2F,
a new hybrid requirements categorization, combining several taxonomies and scales, based on R2F;
Modern software and systems engineering rely heavily on clearly defined requirements to ensure successful development and delivery. The SMART methodology, â€œSystemic Approach to Categorizing and Modeling  Requirementsâ€, provides a structured, rigorous framework to categorize, model and manage requirements throughout the software development lifecycle. This approach offers systematic techniques for categorizing and managing requirements.
Despite its effectiveness, practitioners frequently encounter challenges in implementing the SMART method uniformly due to the absence of specialized digital support. Conventional tools (e.g., requirements editing tools, requirements management tools, spreadsheets) fail to capture the complete semantic depth of SMARTâ€™s categorizations and modeling constructs. A purpose-built software system that implements the SMART methodology can bridge this gap, helping users, mainly software requirements engineers, apply it in a guided, standardized, and automated fashion.
This project proposes to develop software that operationalizes the SMART approach, enabling users to categorize endogenous requirements either new or existing ones.
Endogenous requirements consist of intrinsically describing a generic requirement subject, identified from R2F, by associating it with a specific subject suitable to the future software. The generic subject can be chosen from the R2F subjects, depending on the desired level of refinement, starting from the highest level, comprising three main subjects, to the lowest level, comprising atomic subjects.

1.2 Problem Statement
While the SMART method provides a comprehensive theoretical framework for requirements engineering, there is currently no dedicated software system that operationalizes its concepts in a structured and guided manner. As a result, practitioners rely on general-purpose tools that do not support the SMART categorization process.
The proposed system aims to process and manage endogenous requirements as its primary inputs. These inputs may include:
New requirements entered by users through structured forms.
Existing requirements imported from external sources, such as text documents or requirement repositories.
Metadata describing the requirement context, including:
Requirement textual description (natural language statement),
Associated subject from the R2F framework,
Level of refinement (high-level subject or atomic subject),
These inputs may be provided in structured or semi-structured formats (e.g., form-based entries, text files, or structured requirement records).
The system will apply the SMART (Systemic Approach to Categorizing and Modeling Requirements) methodology to these inputs by:
Mapping each requirement to the appropriate R2F subject hierarchy.
Supporting hybrid categorization from high-level subjects to atomic subjects.
Ensuring consistent classification rules for endogenous requirements.
Allowing users to refine, explore, and retrieve requirements through the SMART taxonomy.
Without such a system, analysts must manually interpret the SMART framework, which often leads to inconsistencies, inefficient categorization, and limited collaboration among team members. Therefore, a dedicated tool is required to systematically support the categorization, management, and exploration of endogenous requirements using the SMART approach.

2.1 Requirements Engineering Background
Requirements engineering is widely recognized as a fundamental area in software engineering. The quality of requirements directly affects system reliability, maintainability, and overall project success. According to the Guide to the Software Engineering Body of Knowledge (SWEBOK), requirement specification, validation, and management are essential activities throughout the software development lifecycle.
Traditional approaches classify requirements into functional and non-functional types which is useful but does not provide enough structure to represent relationships between system components, operational environments, and contextual constraints. As software systems become more complex, this basic classification becomes inadequate for ensuring consistency and clarity in requirement modeling.
Section One: Existing Categories
2.1.1 Category 1: Smart Framework
Smart Framework
The SMART methodology (Systemic Approach to Categorizing and Modeling Requirements) provides an organized way to classify and manage requirements. It builds on the Requirements Reference Framework (R2F), which offers a broad view of software systems by including not only the system itself but also its development and operational environments.
In R2F, requirements are linked to clearly defined subjects that represent different parts of the system. These subjects cover software elements such as functions, data, user interfaces, and technical interfaces, as well as aspects of the development environment like processes, tools, human support, and cognitive factors. It also considers the operational environment, including users, procedures, and conditions under which the system is used. This wide perspective helps in analyzing requirements not just based on functionality, but also in relation to the context in which the system is built and used.
SMART introduces a mixed (hybrid) classification approach that goes beyond simply dividing requirements into functional and non-functional types. Instead, it allows requirements to be categorized across multiple dimensions and levels of detail. Requirements can be broken down step by step, from general concepts to more specific elements, making them clearer and more structured. This process ensures that each requirement is linked to a specific subject within the R2F framework.
One of the main strengths of SMART is its structured and rule-based classification method. Requirements are categorized based on predefined rules and relationships rather than random labeling. This helps reduce confusion and improves consistency. The hierarchical structure also makes it easier to navigate requirements and understand how different parts of the system are connected.
In addition, SMART takes into account quality aspects such as usability, reliability, and safety, along with constraints like technical, economic, and cultural factors. This makes it possible to model requirements in a way that reflects both system behavior and real-world conditions.
However, despite its solid theoretical background, SMART is still mostly conceptual. Its practical use is limited because there are no specialized tools that fully support its classification approach. As a result, analysts often have to apply it manually using general tools, which can lead to inconsistencies and make the process less efficient. To address these limitations, the proposed SMART-S2D system aims to provide a practical implementation of the SMART methodology within a structured and guided software environment.
2.1.2 Category 2: Traditional Requirement Categorization
Traditional Requirement Categorization
Traditional requirement classification usually separates requirements into two main types: Functional and Non-functional. Functional requirements define what a system should do such as handling inputs, producing outputs, or carrying out specific tasks and services. On the other hand, non-functional requirements describe how the system performs these tasks, including qualities like performance, usability, reliability, security, and scalability. This way of classifying requirements is widely used because it is straightforward, easy to understand, and practical in both academic settings and real-world applications.
However, despite how common this approach is, it has clear limitations in terms of depth and structure. It does not clearly show the relationships between different parts of the system, nor does it explain how external factors like technical constraints or operating environments affect the requirements. In many situations, requirements are written at a high level without being broken down into smaller, more precise components.
Because of this, the method often depends heavily on the analystâ€™s judgment when identifying and organizing requirements. This reliance can create inconsistencies, ambiguity, and differences in how various stakeholders interpret the same requirements. In addition, the absence of a clear hierarchy or formal structure makes it harder to trace requirements, ensure consistency, and manage them efficiently especially in complex or large scale systems. These challenges point to the need for more organized and systematic methods of requirement classification.
Comparison Criteria
To compare traditional requirement classification approaches with the SMART framework, the following evaluation criteria are defined. These criteria reflect the level of structure, consistency, and analytical capability provided by each approach:
Basic Categorization (Functional / Non-Functional): The ability to classify requirements into functional (describing system behavior) and non-functional (describing quality attributes and constraints).
Hybrid Taxonomy Structure: The ability to organize requirements using a multi-level classification system that supports hierarchical and cross-dimensional categorization beyond simple binary grouping.
Atomic Subject Refinement: The ability to decompose requirements into precise atomic elements that represent specific system subjects, enabling detailed and unambiguous classification.
Environment Integration: The ability to incorporate development and operational environments (e.g., hardware, software platforms, external systems) into requirement categorization.
Requirement Traceability: The ability to track relationships between requirements and their corresponding system elements, ensuring consistency and facilitating impact analysis.
Categorization Consistency: The degree to which the categorization process produces uniform and repeatable results, either through user discipline or system-guided rules.
Structural Formalization: The level of rigor and formal definition in the categorization process, including the use of models, frameworks, and standardized structures.
Support for Navigation and Retrieval: The ability to browse, explore, and retrieve requirements efficiently through structured navigation mechanisms and search functionalities.

Section Two: Existing Tools
2.1.3 Tool 1: Spreadsheets / Basic Tools
Spreadsheets and basic tools such as Microsoft Excel and Google Sheets are commonly used for documenting and organizing software requirements because of their ease of use and flexibility. These tools allow requirements to be represented in tabular form where each requirement is associated with attributes such as identifier, description, priority, and category. This approach provides a simple and customizable way for analysts to manage requirement data in small-scale projects or early stages of development where formal structure is not required.
However, from a requirement engineering perspective, spreadsheets show significant structural limitations as they lack an embedded conceptual model or taxonomy that ensures consistent categorization of requirements, and classification is typically performed using manually defined labels or columns which introduces ambiguity and inconsistency especially when multiple users are involved or when the project evolves over time, making it more challenging to maintain uniform categorization standards across the dataset.
In terms of structure, spreadsheets do not support hierarchical decomposition or semantic relationships between requirements. They are unable to represent dependencies, contextual constraints, or refinement from high-level subjects to atomic elements. Additionally, linking related requirements or tracking changes across multiple entries is often handled manually which reduces traceability and increases errors.  As a result, maintaining traceability and working with large sets of requirements becomes increasingly difficult as the dataset grows.
Furthermore, spreadsheets do not provide validation mechanisms to ensure the correctness or completeness of requirement categorization. The lack of guided classification forces analysts to rely entirely on manual interpretation which increases the risk of misclassification and reduces consistency across the system. Therefore, while spreadsheets are useful as general tools, they are not suitable for supporting structured and scalable requirement engineering processes. These limitations motivate the use of more advanced requirement management tools such as Jira and IBM DOORS which aim to provide improved organization and traceability.

2.2 Comparative Analysis of Existing Approaches
The comparison highlights the differences between traditional requirement classification, informal tools, structured management systems, and theoretical frameworks for requirement categorization. The traditional functional and non-functional classification provides a simple and widely used way to separate what the system should do from the quality constraints it should satisfy. However, this classification is often too broad and does not provide enough detail to support deeper requirement organization, refinement, or navigation.
Spreadsheets provide a flexible way to document requirements but lack structured categorization, hierarchical organization, and consistency. Tools such as Jira and IBM DOORS improve requirement management through traceability and workflow control, but they rely mainly on user-defined labels and do not enforce a formal categorization model.
In contrast, the SMART framework introduces a more structured and systematic approach based on a hybrid taxonomy and the R2F model. Compared with the traditional functional/non-functional classification, SMART provides more detailed requirement subjects and supports hierarchical categorization, making it easier to classify, refine, and manage requirements consistently. 2.3 Research Gap
Requirement categorization using the SMART methodology is well-supported in theory. The methodology defines clear hierarchical structures and relies on the R2F framework to guide how requirements are classified from within the system itself. These features make it a strong candidate for managing complex requirement sets in a structured and repeatable way.
However, when it comes to actual tool support, the situation is different. No existing requirement management system has been built to reflect how SMART organizes requirements. The hierarchical structures specific to the methodology are not implemented in any current tool, and the logic behind R2F-based endogenous categorization is not enforced anywhere in practice. Because of this, analysts who work with SMART must apply its concepts through manual interpretation, making judgment calls that are not guided or verified by any system.
This creates real problems. When categorization depends on individual interpretation, different analysts will inevitably apply the methodology differently, especially in cases that are not straightforward. Over time, and across larger projects, these differences add up. The consistency that the methodology is supposed to provide starts to break down, and the process becomes harder to manage and scale.
This gap between the theoretical strength of SMART and the lack of tool support for it is the core motivation behind SMART-S2D. The system is not meant to change the methodology itself but to implement it in a way that removes the reliance on manual effort and makes consistent application possible across different users and project contexts.
2.4 Conclusion
In conclusion, this chapter reviewed the main concepts, methods, and tools related to requirement categorization and management. The literature shows that while traditional classifications such as functional and non-functional requirements are widely used, they are often too broad to support detailed categorization and refinement. Existing tools such as spreadsheets, Jira, and IBM DOORS provide useful support for documentation, tracking, and traceability, but they do not enforce a formal requirement categorization methodology.
The SMART methodology, supported by the R2F framework, provides a more structured approach through hierarchical and taxonomy-based categorization. However, the lack of dedicated tool support limits its practical application. Therefore, this research identifies a clear gap between the theoretical strength of SMART and its implementation in real software tools. This gap motivates the development of SMART-S2D as a support system for structured requirement writing, categorization, retrieval, and management

3.6 SEQUENCE DIAGRAMS
3.6.1 Requirement Categorization
Requirement Categorization Sequence Diagram shows how a requirement entered by an analyst is processed and categorized step by step:
The Analyst opens the categorization page in the Web UI and enters requirement details.
The Web UI sends these details to the Categorization Controller.
The controller asks the SMART Engine to suggest atomic (basic) categories using subjects and taxonomy from the Database.
Suggested categories are sent back to the Web UI, which displays them to the analyst.
The analyst selects one category.
The controller then saves the requirement and its chosen category into the Database.
The success confirmation is returned, and the analyst sees the categorized requirement listed in the system.
Figure 9: Requirement Categorization Sequence Diagram
3.6.2 Requirement Retrieval (Multicriteria Search)
This sequence diagram shows how a user (Analyst) searches and views requirements in a system:
The Analyst opens the search page on the Web UI and is shown a search form.
They enter search criteria (like subject, type, priority) and submit it.
The Web UI sends the request to the Search Controller, which queries the Database.
The Database returns matching requirements, which are sent back to the Web UI and displayed to the Analyst.
Optional flow (View Details):
The Analyst selects a specific requirement from the results.
The Web UI requests detailed information from the Search Controller.
The controller fetches the details from the Database and returns them.
The Web UI displays the full requirement details to the Analyst.
In short: it shows the step-by-step interaction between user, UI, backend, and database for searching and viewing requirements.

3.7 DATABASE DIAGRAMS
3.7.1 Entity Relationship
This Entity Relationship Model (ERM) provides a conceptual overview of the systemâ€™s data architecture, focusing on the logical flow from high-level categorization to technical execution. The model is centered around the Software entity, which acts as the primary technical foundation. Generic Subjects are decomposed into Specific Subjects, which in turn serve as the parent categories for individual Requirements; these requirements then link directly to the Software they define. Internally, the software contains various Systems, while externally, it is implemented by one or more Projects. Finally, the Company entity provides organizational oversight by managing these projects. This structure ensures that every requirement is traceable through its specific subject matter to the exact software components and corporate projects responsible for its delivery.
Figure 23: Entity Relationship Diagram
3.7.2 Relational Tables
This relational table model illustrates a structured data flow that connects high-level subjects to technical software implementation and corporate management. Generic Subjects act as parent categories for Specific Subjects, which in turn define the context for individual Requirements through a direct foreign key relationship. These requirements, along with technical Systems, are consolidated under the Software entity, serving as the central technical foundation. Finally, the Project entity integrates the organizational layer by linking a specific piece of software to its managing Company, ensuring that every requirement is traceable from its conceptual origin to its final software delivery and project ownership.
Figure 24: Relational Tables Diagram
3.7.3 Database Scheme
This database schema establishes a structured hierarchy for managing software development by linking high-level categorization to corporate project execution. Specific Subjects are organized under Generic Subjects to provide thematic context for Requirements, which store detailed metadata such as priority and status. These requirements are tied directly to the Software entity, which also serves as a parent to various Systems. Finally, the Project entity integrates the technical and organizational layers by connecting the software to its managing Company, ensuring full traceability from broad subject matter down to specific technical implementations and corporate ownership.

3.8 SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE DIAGRAM
Layer 1: User Layer
The system has four roles Analyst, Project Manager, Admin, and Software User each with different permissions and access levels.
Layer 2: Presentation Layer
The front end interface split into three parts: the Analyst interface for inputting and managing requirements, the admin interface for managing users/projects and monitoring the system, and shared components like login, notifications, and dashboard that all roles use.
Layer 3: Application Layer
The brain of the system where all logic happens, containing six modules: Authentication & Authorization controls access using RBAC, the SMART Categorization Engine automatically classifies requirements using the R2F framework, Requirement Management handles full CRUD and versioning, the Search Engine allows filtering and navigation, the Reporting Module generates XML reports and analytics, and Project & User Management handles assignments and permissions.
Layer 4: Data Layer
The central Database stores everything user info, projects, requirements, SMART categories, logs, reports, and audit records acting as the single source of truth for the whole system.
Layer 5: Integration Layer
Connects the system to the outside world through XML export, authentication services, and future API integrations, while the SMART Knowledge Base provides the taxonomy rules and R2F framework structure that power the categorization engine.
Layer 6: Infrastructure & Security Layer
The foundation that keeps everything running web server, cloud hosting, database server, and backup on the infrastructure side, protected by HTTPS encryption, JWT authentication, input validation, access control, and activity logging on the security side.â€‹â€‹â€‹â€‹â€‹â€‹â€‹â€‹â€‹â€‹â€‹â€‹â€‹â€‹â€‹â€‹

3.9 UI PROTOTYPE
The UI Prototype section presents the visual design and interaction flow of the SMART-S2D system. The purpose of this prototype is to demonstrate how users can manage software requirements using the SMART methodology and the R2F-based requirement categorization approach. The prototype was designed as a high-fidelity web application to show the main screens, navigation structure, requirement-writing workflow, taxonomy selection process, and requirement repository features.
The SMART-S2D prototype focuses on the software requirements branch of endogenous requirements. Since the current project scope is limited to software requirements, the active taxonomy includes four main categories: Function requirements, Data requirements, User Interface requirements, and Technical Interface requirements. Operation requirements and Development requirements are displayed as future taxonomy branches but are disabled in the current prototype. This helps communicate that the system is designed to be expandable while keeping the implemented prototype focused and realistic.
The prototype begins with a workspace page where users can create, join, or open a company and manage related projects. This structure reflects how requirements are usually organized in real software engineering environments, where work belongs to a company or team and each project contains its own requirement repository. Users can create projects, open existing projects, update project information, and manage requirements within the selected project.
After opening a project, users can access the project dashboard, which summarizes requirement information such as total requirements, draft requirements, requirements needing review, approved requirements, and rejected requirements. This dashboard gives project managers, analysts, and other users a quick overview of the requirement status and supports requirement monitoring during the development process.
One of the main interfaces in the prototype is the SMART Requirement Template page. This page guides users through writing requirements using a structured template rather than relying only on free text. The template follows the SMART/R2F writing style by separating the Generic Subject, Specific Subject, Specific Subject statement, and Specific Subject model. This helps users produce clearer and more consistent software requirement statements. The system also provides rule-based validation and category suggestions to support requirement classification without using AI.
The prototype also includes a taxonomy page that visually presents the software requirement taxonomy and allows users to understand how requirements are categorized under the SMART structure. In addition, the requirements repository and search pages allow users to view saved requirements, filter them, open requirement details, and edit requirement information such as title, category, priority, status, and requirement statement. This supports requirement retrieval and refinement, which are important parts of structured requirement management.
The reports page allows users to export project requirements in supported formats such as XML and DOCX. These exports are intended to support academic demonstrations, requirement reviews, and documentation sharing. The Admin/Settings area provides administrative access for managing system-related functions in future development phases.
Overall, the SMART-S2D UI prototype demonstrates a specialized requirement management system rather than a general task management tool. Its interface is designed around SMART-based requirement writing, software requirement taxonomy navigation, structured categorization, project-based organization, and requirement retrieval. By presenting the complete set of prototype screens, the design illustrates how SMART-S2D can help users create, classify, manage, and review software requirements in a more systematic and academically grounded way.
